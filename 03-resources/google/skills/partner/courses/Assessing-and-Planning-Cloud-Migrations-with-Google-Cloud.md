In this lesson, you'll explore the cloud migration value proposition. Before you explore the details of the program, you need to understand why the cloud migration value proposition is important, and how to realize this value from your cloud journey. Cloud adoption presents massive opportunity, with the potential to unlock one trillion dollars in business value. The focus of an organization can be innovation, efficiency or adaptability. For example, consider three companies. Customer A is a startup, and their focus is on innovation. They want to build AI features quickly to beat competitors. Customer B, an old bank, is focused on efficiency. They want to stop spending millions on old data centers. And Customer C, a retailer, is focused on adaptability because they are worried about their website crashing on Black Friday. Regardless of the focus, be it innovation, efficiency, or adaptability, the investment required is significant. However, this investment is justified by the returns, with well-run migrations yielding an average Return on Investment or ROI benefit of 180 percent. To realize the value of cloud migration, you must map the customer journey across three distinct paths. The mapping can be viewed as a strategic assessment. You analyze the customer's current situation and sort everything into three neat paths, or categories, to ensure nothing gets left behind or is moved incorrectly. It is important to note that across all three of these paths, the Security and Resilience Framework is used to ensure security is embedded from the start. Let's explore each one in detail. First is Application Upgrading. This focuses on migrating workloads like Java and . NET applications. Think of this as a home renovation: rather than simply moving an old, inefficient appliance into a brand-new smart home, you upgrade to a modern model that fits the new environment. In the cloud, you apply that same logic. The goal is to improve the developer experience by upgrading to serverless and cloud-native technologies such as, Cloud Run and GKE, and securing the software supply chain. You use the Cloud App Modernization Program, also known as CAMP for this specific journey. CAMP has been designed as an end-to-end framework to help guide organizations through their modernization journey by assessing where they are today and identifying their most effective path forward. Second is Data Upgrading. This involves migrating databases such as Oracle, SQL Server, and Postgres, away from self-managed servers. Think of this like moving your assets from a home safe to a bank. Instead of worrying about the locks, security, and maintenance yourself, you let the bank handle the logistics while you focus on how to use the assets. In the cloud, you achieve this by moving to fully managed Relational Database Management Systems or RDBMS capabilities like Cloud SQL, AlloyDB, and Spanner. This approach drastically reduces operational overhead and is often referred to as 'Move to Managed'. Third is Infrastructure Upgrading, which is the primary focus of the Rapid Migration and Modernization Program, also known as RaMP. Think of this as securing the foundation. Just as a house needs a solid base to support its furniture and appliances, your applications and data need a robust environment to run effectively. Core Microsoft and Linux environments are migrated to destinations like Compute Engine and Google Cloud VMware Engine, using tools such as Migration Center to perform this efficiently. You will learn more about RaMP later. Mapping the journey across three paths is the first step, but generating value requires momentum. To turn these isolated upgrades into a self-sustaining engine of growth, you can use the 'Flywheel' approach. This ensures that every upgrade you make, whether in Applications, Data, or Infrastructure, fuels the next one, creating a cycle of continuous improvement rather than a one-time fix. The first phase is Opportunity Evaluation. Carefully assess the customer's environment. If there is no clear business value, securing stakeholder support is difficult. Google provides tools to help build a solid business case, or enhance an existing one, to ensure executive alignment early on. The second phase is Preparation and Planning. This is where you identify and prioritize workloads. Google has made this easier with the Google Cloud Setup checklists found in the console. A best practice here is to configure your settings and download them as Terraform. This allows you to manage your foundation using Infrastructure as Code, which is much more scalable than clicking through a portal. The third phase is Migrate and Validate. Doing migrations manually is tedious and risky. Migrate to Virtual Machines enables you to migrate your VMs, with minor automatic modifications, from your source environment. It supports migrating VMs from VMware, AWS, and Azure directly to Compute Engine. This toolset allows us to automate the process rather than relying on custom scripts that might break if the author leaves the company. The final phase is Operate and Optimize. This is where you deliver the long-term value the business expects. By using cloud-native tools to optimize the environment, you gain experience that accelerates future migrations. In this lesson, you'll explore the various migration strategies, followed by a discussion on selecting the most appropriate strategy. The lesson also examines the business drivers and the "6 Rs of Modernization" framework used to prioritize and select the best approach for different workloads. Let's begin by exploring the various migration approaches at a high level. One approach is to take existing infrastructure and move it to the cloud without making any significant changes. This is suitable when the on-premises workload is in an acceptable form for running and managing in the cloud, similar to a VM-to-VM migration. The benefit of this "move" approach is the minimal changes required. A second approach, change or replatforming, is necessary when a simple 'Lift and Shift' won't work. This happens when the cloud environment doesn't support specific legacy constraints, such as outdated kernel versions or proprietary hardware dependencies. Changes must be made to the underlying infrastructure or configuration to adapt the application. This isn't a total code rewrite, but it's more than a copy-paste job. However, customers often choose this path voluntarily because it delivers immediate return on investment in the form of improved scalability, availability, security, or durability. The third approach involves deciding that the current application is not worth migrating, leading to a "get rid of everything and start afresh" approach. Benefits include creating a cloud-native solution, but challenges include needing the expertise and the potential for a longer timeline. While this is often the desired outcome, it's not always possible. The drivers influencing the choice of migration approach are important to explore. Some legacy, monolithic, or custom off-the-shelf applications might not be designed for the cloud. Migrating these to the cloud might be too challenging, making them better suited for an on-premises environment. Generally, cloud services are easier to manage and use, mostly because they are more modern. Working in the cloud offers many benefits in terms of time to value and the ability to achieve more with less effort, provided the necessary knowledge is available. A challenge on-premises is the difficulty of capacity planning due to the challenge of predicting the future. However, there are times when running certain workloads outside of the cloud is the only option. Customers may want to move off an on-premises facility. This can drive the need to migrate legacy workloads to the public cloud without modification, such as with a lift-and-shift VM-to-VM migration. This represents a shift from a Capex (capital expenditure) to an Opex (operating expense) model, as customers prefer the pay- as-you-go or pay-per-use model offered by the cloud. The cloud also addresses capacity planning through resource pooling, allowing for scaling up and down based on resource needs. Cloud providers bill based on allocated resources or even only when the application is running. The security argument is acknowledged by those familiar with cloud security, though those new to it may struggle. For organizations targeting microservices and cloud-native agility, Google enables a true hybrid and multi-cloud approach through the Google Distributed Cloud portfolio. You can run Google Kubernetes Engine directly on-premises, whether on bare metal or VMware - transforming your local infrastructure into a portable, cloud-native platform. This environment is fully manageable using Infrastructure as Code tools like Terraform, and it supports advanced governance through Policy Controller. This allows you to enforce consistent guardrails across all your clusters, even those running locally, simply by defining your policies in a centralized Git repository. However, the greatest advantages come from running workloads natively in the cloud. This unlocks serverless platforms like Cloud Run, where Google manages the servers entirely, removing that burden from your team. Google also revolutionized Kubernetes by introducing GKE Autopilot, a mode where we manage the nodes for you. All of this runs on Google's differentiator: a single, global network that connects every data center. This allows you to route data a lot closer to your users achieving very low latency, while our global load balancers distribute traffic but also provide inherent protection against DDoS attacks. While migration often starts as a financial shift from CapEx to OpEx, leveraging these cloud-native capabilities delivers value far beyond a simple 'lift and shift. With these business drivers in mind, let's explore the migration approaches in more detail now starting with the most common strategy, "lift and shift". Lift and shift involves moving workloads from a source environment, such as on-premises or VMware engine, to Compute Engine. This strategy doesn't require excessive effort which is ideal when applications can run unmodified in the cloud, a fast migration is essential, and there is little desire or need for change. Recall that Google provides the "Migrate to Virtual Machines" tool to automate and simplify this process. You choose lift and shift when you don't want to change much, are content running the application as virtual machines in the cloud, and want to get things done quickly. For example, migrating hundreds of applications can take just minutes. Lift and shift involves migrating on-premises workloads to the cloud as virtual machines with virtually no modification. While this immediately unlocks the financial flexibility of a pay-as-you-go model, the underlying application architecture remains identical you are effectively swapping your own data center hardware for Google's infrastructure Let's now explore an example architecture of the "lift and shift" strategy, specifically the environment before migration. Notice that the traditional on-premises setup involves an app server, web server, load balancer, and a MySQL database all within a Data Center. This classic workload configuration requires on-site management. You often apply the "lift and shift" approach to non-consequential applications, which allows the business to focus modernization efforts on more critical systems. For instance, an on-premises software or hardware load balancer becomes the managed and serverless HTTP global load balancer in the cloud, and the application, web, and database servers utilize Compute Engine using a lift and shift. The components are deployed across Regions and Zones for availability. This transition is simple because the architecture remains largely the same, making the shift to Compute Engine straightforward for those familiar with virtual machines. The "lift and shift" approach offers several benefits and some drawbacks. These migrations are the easiest to perform because your team can continue to use the same set of tools and skills that they were using before. These migrations also support ready-made software. Because you migrate existing workloads with minimal refactoring, "lift and shift" migrations tend to be the quickest. However, after migration, the workloads that are running in the target environment might not be optimized for the cloud. These workloads don't take full advantage of cloud platform features, such as horizontal scalability, fine-grained pricing, and highly managed services. Even in a 'Lift and Shift,' compatibility is key. The 'adjustments' mentioned here usually refer to OS-level changes rather than code rewriting. We are talking about swapping out underlying storage drivers, updating network interfaces, and removing legacy hardware dependencies. This is an essential step to ensure that the application is completely compatible and operates successfully after the workload is moved. While Lift and Shift involves minimal changes, the next migration approach, 'Change,' is necessary when you need to upgrade applications before or during migration. This second strategy is known as "Move and Improve," also sometimes referred to as "Replatform". The approach depends on whether you can move the workload to the cloud first and then improve it, or if you must improve it before you can move it. The key point is that the workload needs to be adapted in some way to run in the cloud. For example, suppose you have a virtual machine and want to run it as a container instead. This involves a developer-centric process using tools provided by Google. The tools include a script called the "Migrate to Containers CLI" and a plug-in bundle for different operating systems. The bundle generates artifacts like a Dockerfile and all the YAML files. These files are necessary to implement a Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment (or CI/CD) pipeline for building and deploying the container image. These artifacts are automatically generated based on a structured approach. This approach is also "improve and move" because you are running it as a Docker container, not a virtual machine. You start with a VM, potentially running on-premises, but instead of moving it as a VM, you generate a Docker container image and run it as a container in a serverless or GKE platform. This allows you to benefit from container-based capabilities. Let's return to the example architecture of the environment before migration. Once again the traditional on-premises setup involves an app server, web server, load balancer, and a MySQL database all within a Data Center. Even if you are migrating and running the application within a virtual machine, the "improve and move" approach allows you to take advantage of cloud-native capabilities like managed instance groups. You can think of the lateral move (pure lift and shift) as a one-to-one mapping (namely, one VM on-premises equals one VM in the cloud). Moving toward a cloud-native architecture starts with optimizing the infrastructure. We can leverage Managed Instance Groups to introduce elasticity and autohealing to standard VMs. But VMs remain heavy and platform-dependent. To achieve true portability, we must refactor into Containers. Containerization is the key enabler for modern platforms like GKE Autopilot and Serverless Cloud Run. These services abstract the underlying infrastructure entirely, allowing us to focus on the four key pillars of modernization: portability, massive scalability, inherent high availability, and simplified management. The "Move and improve" migration strategy offers several benefits, primarily resulting in a more cloud-optimized solution that is scalable, highly available, and provides greater environmental consistency. However, this approach is more complex than a simple "lift and shift." Its drawbacks include often requiring application refactoring, making it potentially unsuitable for certain off-the-shelf applications, and demanding investment in new tools and updated skill sets for the team. Furthermore, it necessitates a re-evaluation of existing plans and takes longer to complete than the "Lift and Shift" approach. The third approach is "Rip and Replace," where you decide to implement a fresh solution. Rip and Replace involves completely rewriting an application to be cloud-native, developed specifically for, and in, the cloud environment. This is the most drastic strategy. It is chosen when the current application is critical but failing to meet its goals, or when it is no longer technically or financially worth maintaining or migrating. This path is chosen by customers who anticipate exponential growth that legacy architectures, such as those based on Virtual Machines, cannot support, or when their industry is undergoing rapid transformation. The process involves ditching the current implementation entirely to build a new solution using cloud-native patterns like microservices and deploying them directly to the cloud. Let's return to the on-premises setup again. This strategy moves beyond simple replication, focusing instead on refactoring your infrastructure to become truly cloud-native. Here, you divide monolithic applications into microservices, achieving inherent geo-redundancy through Cloud DNS and global Load Balancers fronted by Cloud CDN. You also shed the burden of manual maintenance by adopting managed services like Cloud Bigtable and Cloud SQL. While this deep transformation represents a large change to your operating model a, it typically requires longer term thinking and investment. The "Rip and Replace" approach offers the most significant advantages for transformation, as it allows organizations to fully leverage cloud-native capabilities, effectively removing technical debt and enabling things that were previously impossible. The resulting applications are typically easily scalable, highly available, and highly managed. However, this strategy is not without its drawbacks. It doesn't work for off-the-shelf applications and requires a significant code rewrite, demanding a substantial investment of time and effort. It also necessitates acquiring new tools and updating the skill sets of the team to understand cloud-native capabilities, along with a complete re-evaluation of existing plans. Despite the high investment, if executed successfully, the payoff in business value can be significant. While the broad strategies of Moving, Changing, or Replacing provide a helpful high-level view, they are only the starting point. It's important to recognize that one size does not fit all, and you must analyze your specific objectives and environment to select the right approach. This leads you to the industry-standard "6 Rs of Modernization." This comprehensive framework allows you to select the precise tactic for every workload: Rehosting, Replatforming, Refactoring, Repurchasing, Retiring unneeded assets, or Retaining applications on-premises when migration is not yet feasible. The 6 Rs of migration provide a clear, structured way to think about how applications move during a cloud transformation. They range from Rehost, which lifts existing workloads with minimal change, through Replatform and Refactor, which progressively introduce cloud-native optimisation and architectural modernisation. Beyond technical change, the framework also recognises strategic choices: Repurchase replaces legacy systems with SaaS, Retire removes applications that no longer deliver value, and Retain deliberately keeps certain workloads in place where migration is not yet justified. Together, the 6 Rs help organisations balance speed, risk, cost, and long-term business outcomes when planning and executing migrations. Determining the right strategy involves a decision tree process guided by questions like whether the application can move, should move, or requires changes. Applications are then categorized as, Retired, Retained, Rehosted, Replatformed, Refactored, or Replaced based on the answers to these questions to inform the overall migration strategy and priority.

The sprint approach enables a rapid start by focusing on "low-hanging fruit" or quick wins initially, with the process refining along the way as your team gains experience. The example displayed now depicts the activities typically performed in a Migration Sprint. The sprint begins with Discover, where you select migration candidates and define pre and post-migration steps. Throughout the process, you constantly revisit the assessment and review the backlog to ensure information is valid and workloads are prioritized. Next, you Prepare by executing pre-migration tasks and testing, starting with a test to ensure the application can migrate and run in the cloud. Once the migration is validated, you Migrate, performing the actual cutover, which could involve hundreds of VMs, and executing post-migration tasks. Following the cutover, you Test and Verify by conducting User Acceptance Testing and regression testing, ensuring everything was migrated properly. Finally, you Optimize by reducing overheads through resized or refactored infrastructure, and you Improve the process by learning lessons for subsequent sprints. At the end, you may also archive what was running on-premises. The workflow within a sprint follows a specific pattern that begins by Selecting applications from the backlog. Next, you Prepare by analyzing them to validate technical requirements and assumptions. This is followed by a test migration to prove cloud compatibility before you Migrate the actual production workloads. Afterward, you Test and Verify to validate success through User Acceptance Testing and regression testing. Thereafter, Improve the process by capturing lessons learned and feeding them into the next wave, while also focusing on Operations and Optimization to obtain additional value from the workloads running in the cloud. While the initial discovery supports the overall business case, the additional discovery in every sprint ensures you have all the technical information necessary for success. This phase, which is the most time-consuming part of the sprint, involves crossing the deliverables from the initial assessment with real-time dependencies and application state to compile a comprehensive migration runbook. The key outputs include dependency mapping, scope refinement, and the testing plan, which define the necessary foundations preparation, pre and post-migration operations activities, and the final migration runbook and automation. Migration Factory is designed for scale, with the sweet spot being large, complex migration programs involving 50 to hundreds of VMs, making it less suitable for your small-scale migrations. On the positive side, you gain several pros, including increased migration velocity, less financial burden over time, shortened risk time, and improved quality. However, you should be aware that this approach can also be more complex and involve higher initial costs and effort. With the workloads successfully migrated, you arrive at the final phase of the methodology: Optimize. A key pillar of the Optimize phase is the Modernization Factory, a developer-led approach focused on transforming and significantly changing applications. It's typically used for a smaller volume of applications that represent the most differentiating aspects of the business. It focuses on the business value derived from the cloud transformation by delivering training and sharing best practices, which in turn empowers development teams to perform these complex migrations. Modernization Factory integrates directly into the broader cloud migration program, focusing on the Assess and Plan phases for specific workloads. This approach is designed for the most transformative migration strategies, namey, Replace and Refactor. For applications undergoing a Replace strategy, the Modernization Factory guides the complete transformation, which can mean transforming and upgrading the application or using new Commercial Off-The-Shelf or Software as a Service solutions to substitute legacy systems. It also enables the Refactor (or re-architect) approach, which involves significant application changes to fully leverage cloud-native capabilities. It's important to distinguish between the Migration Factory and the Modernization Factory. To demonstrate the cost-saving migration path, let's focus on business case 1, which centers on objectives like a data center exit and avoiding hardware/software renewals. This path involves a Lift-and-Shift migration, which is accomplished using Migration Factory and replication-based migration tools. It's a rapid approach that results in only a small change to the existing operating model. This path is suitable for customers who want to modernize their applications and move to cloud-native solutions, typically with a time limit of one, or a maximum of two, years. The Migration Factory focuses on VM migration (Lift and Shift). This approach scales from a few VMs to hundreds and is ideal for large, complex programs like data center decommissioning. It addresses cost savings regarding hardware, operations, and licensing. You can migrate VMware to VMware Engine or Compute Engine, replatform to containers, or simply retire or retain applications. This aligns with the Cloud Adoption Framework pillars: Learn, Lead, Scale, and Secure. Finally, explore the Modernization Factory path, represented here as Business Case 2. Unlike the rapid lift-and-shift approach, this is a developer-centric strategy focused on deep transformation - refactoring monolithic applications into cloud-native microservices or containers, often using GKE Enterprise. By leveraging CI/CD pipelines as the primary migration tool, you are fundamentally changing your operating model to avoid further investment in legacy infrastructure. While this path requires a longer commitment, typically three to five years, it delivers the highest level of agility and future-proofing for your most critical systems.
